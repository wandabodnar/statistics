About this tutorial

This tutorial follows the broad learning progression in Mark Gardener’s Statistics for Ecologists Using R and Excel: Data Collection, Exploration, Analysis and Presentation (2nd edition).

The book is organised around the scientific process:

planning → recording → exploration → analysis → reporting

That structure is retained here because statistical analysis should begin with a scientific question, a sampling design and a clear understanding of the data.

The original book uses both Excel and R. This tutorial keeps the conceptual sequence but uses a modern R workflow built around:

readr → dplyr → tidyr → ggplot2 → base R statistics

The scientific method

Scientific research is a careful and systematic investigation of the natural or social world. It can improve existing knowledge, test established ideas, identify patterns and relationships, or generate new knowledge. Scientific conclusions should be based on evidence collected and analysed using transparent and reproducible methods.

A scientific investigation generally involves:

  • Observing a pattern or developing an idea;

  • Expressing that idea as a research question or hypothesis;

    • a hypothesis is a testable explanation or prediction based on existing knowledge or observations;
    • where appropriate, state both:
      • the null hypothesis: no difference, effect or relationship;
      • the alternative hypothesis: a difference, effect or relationship exists;

  • Deciding how the question or hypothesis can be tested through an appropriate study or experimental design;

    • define the sampling units, variables and measurements;
    • decide how observations will be replicated;
    • consider whether observations are independent;
    • use the resulting evidence to either:
      • reject the null hypothesis, or
      • fail to reject the null hypothesis;
    • scientific evidence can support a hypothesis, but it does not usually prove it with absolute certainty;

  • Collecting a suitable and representative sample;

    • population: the larger group or process about which we want to make an inference
    • sample: the subset of observations actually collected from that population or process;
    • the aim is for the sample to represent the population sufficiently well for the intended inference;
ImportantPotential problems
  • even a randomly selected sample may, by chance, represent the population poorly;
  • natural ecological variation may obscure an underlying effect or pattern;
  • inadequate sample size, biased sampling or non-independent observations can weaken inference;

  • Analysing the data using appropriate statistical methods;

  • Evaluating how strongly the evidence supports the original hypothesis or explanation;

    • Consider estimated effect: how big the pattern, difference, or relationship is.
    • Consider uncertainty: how precise that estimate is. This is often expressed using a standard error or confidence interval.
    • Consider statistical evidence: how compatible the data are with a null hypothesis or statistical model. This can include a p-value, likelihood ratio test, AIC comparison, or other model-based evidence.
    • Consider alternative explanations: other processes that could produce the same observed pattern.

  • Refining the explanation and generating new questions where necessary.

Science is therefore iterative. A statistical result is not the end of the process. It contributes evidence, changes what we know, and often generates new questions.

Descriptive vs Inferential Statistics

Descriptive statistics are used to summarise and describe the data that were actually collected. They help us understand the main features of a sample before attempting to draw wider conclusions. Examples include the number or percentage of observations in different categories, the mean or median, measures of variation such as the standard deviation or range, and graphical summaries such as histograms or boxplots.

Inferential statistics are used to move beyond the observed sample and make inferences about a larger population or ecological process. They help us assess whether an observed difference, relationship or pattern is likely to reflect something more general rather than variation specific to the sample that happened to be collected.

Descriptive and inferential statistics therefore serve different but complementary purposes. Descriptive statistics tell us what the sample looks like; inferential statistics help us evaluate what the sample may tell us about the wider population or process.

Descriptive analysis usually comes first. After data have been collected, they should be inspected, summarised and visualised before formal statistical models or hypothesis tests are applied. This helps reveal patterns, variability, unusual observations and potential problems in the dataset.

Types of ecological project

Descriptive projects

Summarise a situation.

Examples:

What is the average fish abundance?

How variable is salinity?

How many species were recorded?

No formal hypothesis test is necessarily required.

Useful tools include:

  • means;
  • medians;
  • standard deviations;
  • ranges;
  • confidence intervals;
  • graphs;
  • diversity metrics.

Difference projects

Compare groups.

Examples:

Is abundance different between two habitats?

Does richness differ among monitoring sites?

Common methods include:

  • t-test;
  • Mann–Whitney U-test;
  • paired tests;
  • ANOVA;
  • Kruskal–Wallis.

Correlation and regression projects

To investigate a relationship between numerical variables.

Examples:

Does fish abundance change with salinity?

Does richness change through time?

Common methods include:

  • Spearman correlation;
  • Pearson correlation;
  • linear regression;
  • multiple regression.

Association projects

To investigate links between categorical variables.

Example:

Is fish presence associated with habitat category?

Common methods include:

  • contingency tables;
  • chi-squared tests;
  • goodness-of-fit tests.

Community studies

Whole-community data require additional methods.

Examples include:

  • diversity;
  • similarity;
  • dissimilarity;
  • ordination;
  • multivariate methods.

These are treated briefly later in this tutorial and in much greater depth in a dedicated community analysis workflow.

Hypotheses and null hypotheses

A useful hypothesis should be specific enough to test.

Weak:

Fish abundance differs through time.

More useful:

Fish abundance has declined through time.

The null hypothesis generally represents no pattern or no difference.

For example:

\[ H_0: \text{there is no temporal trend in fish abundance} \]

The alternative might be:

\[ H_1: \text{fish abundance changes through time} \]

or, if there is strong prior justification:

\[ H_1: \text{fish abundance declines through time} \]

NoteDirectional prediction versus statistical test

A biologically directional prediction does not mean you should automatically use a one-sided test.

The scientific prediction and the formal test need to be considered separately.

Data types

The type of data determines what analyses are meaningful.

There are three broad categories: interval, ordinal and categorical.

For modern R work it is also helpful to distinguish counts and binary variables explicitly.

Numerical continuous

Values can take many values along a scale.

Examples:

  • temperature;
  • salinity;
  • fish length;
  • dissolved oxygen.

R commonly stores these as:

double / numeric

Numerical discrete

Whole-number counts.

Examples:

  • fish abundance;
  • species richness;
  • number of surveys.

R may store these as:

integer

Counts often require special probability distributions in statistical models.

Ordinal

Categories have a meaningful order but unequal or unknown spacing.

Examples:

poor < moderate < good

or:

rare < occasional < frequent < abundant

Categorical nominal

Categories differ but have no natural ranking.

Examples:

  • monitoring site;
  • habitat type;
  • species;
  • sampling method.

In R these are often represented as factors.

Binary

Two possible states.

Examples:

present / absent
yes / no
0 / 1

Binary responses are particularly important in ecological occurrence models.

Sampling effort and representativeness

A sample is intended to tell us something about a larger population or process.

The key question is:

Is the sample representative enough for the inference I want to make?

Replication

Replication allows variation to be estimated.

One observation may describe one event, but it gives almost no information about natural variability.

Sample size

Sample size is the number of independent observational units contributing information to an analysis.

For example:

20 independent surveys
→ n = 20

Sample size matters because it affects how precisely we can estimate ecological patterns.

Small sample:

  • estimates are more variable;
  • confidence intervals are wider;
  • unusual observations have more influence;
  • statistical tests have less power to detect real effects.

Larger sample:

  • estimates are usually more stable;
  • confidence intervals become narrower;
  • patterns are easier to distinguish from random variation;
  • the analysis has greater statistical power.

Standard error

As sample size n increases, the standard error generally becomes smaller. This means the estimated mean becomes more precise.

Statistical power

Statistical power is the probability of detecting a real effect when that effect actually exists.

Power generally increases when:

  • sample size increases;
  • the true effect is larger;
  • variability is lower;
  • the design is more efficient.

A study with very small sample size may fail to detect an ecologically meaningful effect simply because there is too much uncertainty.

Ecological independence

In ecological datasets, the most important question is:

How many independent sampling units do I have?

ImportantMain learning goal

Very large sample sizes can make very small effects statistically significant.

With large n, always examine:

  • effect size;
  • confidence interval;
  • ecological meaning.

Sampling effort

More effort generally gives a better picture of the system, but effort must also be comparable.

Examples:

  • number of seine hauls;
  • duration of kick sampling;
  • quadrat area;
  • number of visits.

Random sampling

Sampling locations are selected using a random process.

Goal:

reduce systematic selection bias.

Systematic sampling

Samples are collected according to a structured design.

This is especially useful along environmental gradients.

Example:

sampling from upper to lower estuary.

Mixed sampling

Random and systematic elements are combined.

Haphazard sampling

Sampling is not formally random or systematic.

This may sometimes be unavoidable, but limitations should be recognised.

Independence and pseudoreplication

A major modern extension to basic test selection is the need to ask whether observations are truly independent.

Suppose you have:

10 surveys at Richmond
10 surveys at Kew
10 surveys at Greenwich

You do not necessarily have 30 fully independent observations.

Surveys from the same site may resemble one another because they share:

  • habitat;
  • geography;
  • salinity regime;
  • local environmental conditions.

Repeated surveys within the same year may also share conditions.

This is hierarchical data - and this connects to the use of mixed models.

Data recording

A biological record should contain enough information to remain understandable later.

At minimum, think:

  • what was measured;
  • where it was measured;
  • when it was measured;
  • how it was measured;
  • which sampling event it belongs to.

A tidy ecological dataset often looks like:

SURVEY_ID SITE YEAR MONTH SPECIES_NAME FISH_COUNT
S001 Richmond 1992 9 Smelt 12
S001 Richmond 1992 9 Flounder 3
S002 Kew 1992 9 Smelt 7

Each row is one record.

Each column is one variable.

Derive a survey-level abundance response

The raw dataset contains multiple species rows within each survey.

To analyse total fish abundance, first calculate one abundance value per survey.

survey_abundance_observed <- fish_raw %>%
  group_by(SURVEY_ID) %>%
  summarise(
    Abundance = sum(
      FISH_COUNT,
      na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop")

Create the survey universe:

survey_universe <- fish_raw %>%
  distinct(
    SURVEY_ID,
    SITE,
    YEAR,
    MONTH)

Join:

survey_abundance <- survey_universe %>%
  left_join(
    survey_abundance_observed,
    by = "SURVEY_ID") %>%
  mutate(
    Abundance = replace_na(
      Abundance,
      0))
WarningMissing does not generally mean zero

replace_na(Abundance, 0) is appropriate here only because the complete survey universe has independently established that the survey occurred.

Never convert missing values to zero automatically.

Standard deviation versus standard error

These are often confused.

Standard deviation describes variation among observations.

Standard error describes uncertainty in an estimated mean.

For a simple independent sample:

\[ SE = \frac{SD}{\sqrt{n}} \]

In R:

abundance_sd <- sd(survey_abundance$Abundance)

abundance_n <- sum(
  !is.na(
    survey_abundance$Abundance))

abundance_se <- abundance_sd / sqrt(abundance_n)

abundance_se
[1] 3.282056
ImportantDo not use SE as if it described biological variation

Small standard error can coexist with substantial ecological variability if the sample size is large.

Confidence intervals

A confidence interval expresses uncertainty around an estimated parameter.

For a simple mean:

mean_abundance <- mean(survey_abundance$Abundance)

ci_lower <-
  mean_abundance -
  qt(
    0.975,
    df = abundance_n - 1) *
  abundance_se

ci_upper <-
  mean_abundance +
  qt(
    0.975,
    df = abundance_n - 1) *
  abundance_se

c(
  Lower = ci_lower,

Normal distribution

Many classical parametric tests are derived under assumptions involving normally distributed errors or observations.

A normal distribution is:

  • symmetric;
  • bell-shaped;
  • characterised by a mean and standard deviation.

But do not confuse:

normality of the response

with:

normality of model residuals

For regression and ANOVA, assumptions generally concern the residuals.

Q–Q plots

A Q–Q plot is usually more informative than relying only on a formal normality test.

qqnorm(survey_abundance$Abundance)

qqline(survey_abundance$Abundance)

If the points closely follow the line, the distribution is broadly compatible with normality.

Shapiro–Wilk test

Used to ask whether a set of values departs significantly from a normal distribution.

shapiro.test(survey_abundance$Abundance)

    Shapiro-Wilk normality test

data:  survey_abundance$Abundance
W = 0.93599, p-value = 7.116e-10

Interpret cautiously.

With large datasets, small deviations from normality can become statistically significant even when they are not practically important.

Therefore combine:

  • graphical inspection;
  • model diagnostics;
  • ecological understanding.

Choosing the correct analysis

Before choosing a test, ask:

What is the question?
        ↓
What is the response variable?
        ↓
How many groups or predictors?
        ↓
Are observations independent?
        ↓
What is the distribution / data type?
        ↓
What assumptions does the method require?

A simplified classical guide:

Difference

Two-group differences: t-test

The independent-samples t-test compares means between two groups with approximately Gaussian distribution, also called a normal distribution or bell curve.

Example dataset:

two_sites <- survey_abundance %>%
  filter(
    SITE %in% c(
      "Richmond",
      "Kew"))

Plot first:

ggplot(
  two_sites,
  aes(x = SITE, y = Log_Abundance)) +
  geom_boxplot() +
  geom_jitter(
    width = 0.1,
    alpha = 0.5) +
  theme_classic()

Fit:

t.test(
  Log_Abundance ~ SITE,
  data = two_sites)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  Log_Abundance by SITE
t = -0.53113, df = 71.111, p-value = 0.597
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Kew and group Richmond is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -0.2605642  0.1509465
sample estimates:
     mean in group Kew mean in group Richmond 
              1.829119               1.883928

R uses Welch’s t-test by default, which does not assume equal variances.

What the t-test asks

\[ H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 \]

The test asks whether the observed difference in sample means is larger than would be expected from sampling variability under the null hypothesis.

What the p-value does not tell you

A small p-value does not tell you:

  • the ecological importance of the difference;
  • the probability that the null hypothesis is true;
  • the magnitude of the effect.

Always inspect effect size and uncertainty.

Two-group differences: Mann–Whitney U-test

For two non-parametric, independent groups where a rank-based comparison is more appropriate:

wilcox.test(
  Abundance ~ SITE,
  data = two_sites)

    Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction

data:  Abundance by SITE
W = 929, p-value = 0.9058
alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

In R this is implemented using wilcox.test().

The test is based on ranks rather than raw values.

It is not simply a “test of medians” in every situation; its interpretation depends on distributional assumptions.

Paired tests

Paired data arise when observations are naturally matched.

Examples:

  • before versus after at the same site;
  • upstream versus downstream paired locations;
  • same experimental unit under two conditions.

#   after,

#   before,
#   after,
#   paired = TRUE)

Reporting statistical results

Statistical reporting should answer four questions:

  1. What was analysed?
  2. What was the estimated pattern or difference?
  3. How uncertain was the estimate?
  4. What statistical evidence supports it?

Weak:

Abundance significantly declined.

Better:

Model-adjusted fish abundance declined through time, with an estimated annual change of X% (95% CI: X to X%; p < 0.001).

Report effect sizes, not only p-values

A p-value answers a limited question about compatibility with a null model.

It does not tell you whether an effect is biologically important.

Whenever possible report:

  • coefficient;
  • difference;
  • correlation;
  • odds ratio;
  • percentage change;
  • confidence interval;
  • relevant test statistic;
  • p-value.

Statistical significance versus biological importance

Consider:

Effect A:
change = 0.1%
p < 0.001

Effect B:
change = 15%
p = 0.08

Effect A is statistically precise but may be ecologically trivial.

Effect B may be biologically important but uncertain.

Scientific interpretation requires both magnitude and uncertainty.

Reporting graphs

A good figure should:

  • answer a clear question;
  • have labelled axes;
  • show units;
  • display uncertainty where relevant;
  • avoid unnecessary decoration;
  • be readable without excessive explanation.

Do not use a graph merely because software can produce it.

Writing the Results section

Results should primarily describe evidence.

A useful paragraph structure is:

  1. state the main pattern;
  2. give the effect size;
  3. give uncertainty;
  4. give the test/model statistic;
  5. refer to the figure or table.

Avoid explaining ecological mechanisms in detail here. That belongs mainly in the Discussion.

Writing the Discussion

A strong Discussion separates:

Result
↓
Biological interpretation
↓
Comparison with literature
↓
Alternative explanations
↓
Limitations
↓
Implications

For every interpretation ask:

Did my analysis actually test this?

Critical thinking

Observation versus interpretation

Example:

Observation

Fish abundance declined through time.

Interpretation

Environmental conditions may have become less favourable.

Causal claim

Declining oxygen caused the abundance decline.

The first may be directly supported.

The second may be plausible.

The third requires direct evidence.

Do not collapse these three levels.

Alternative explanations

Whenever you favour an explanation, ask:

What else could produce the same pattern?

For long-term fish monitoring:

  • sampling month;
  • gear differences;
  • sampling effort;
  • taxonomic changes;
  • extreme recruitment years;
  • site composition;
  • habitat alteration;
  • salinity change;
  • temperature;
  • water quality;
  • stochastic variability.

Ask whether competing explanations have been eliminated.

What would change my mind?

This is a powerful scientific habit.

Ask:

What observation would weaken my preferred explanation?

If no possible evidence could change your conclusion, the conclusion is not being treated scientifically.

Modern analysis should not be reduced to:

normal → parametric
not normal → non-parametric

Instead ask:

  • Is the response distribution appropriate?
  • Are residuals behaving reasonably?
  • Is variance changing?
  • Is there overdispersion or underdispersion?
  • Are there excess zeros?
  • Is the random-effects structure supported?
  • Does the model represent the design?

A modern ecological analysis workflow

  1. Define the biological question.
  2. Define the sampling unit.
  3. Identify response type.
  4. Identify predictors.
  5. Identify hierarchy/repeated sampling.
  6. Explore the raw data.
  7. Plot the response.
  8. Decide whether transformation or a non-Gaussian family is appropriate.
  9. Fit a biologically plausible model.
  10. Diagnose assumptions.
  11. Compare appropriate candidate models if needed.
  12. Estimate effects and uncertainty.
  13. Back-transform where required.
  14. Visualise adjusted effects.
  15. Interpret biological meaning.
  16. Consider alternative explanations.
  17. Report effect size, CI and statistical evidence.

Common mistakes

Choosing the test before defining the question

Start with biology.

Ignoring sampling design

Repeated observations are not automatically independent.

Testing raw-response normality mechanically

For models, residual behaviour is usually more relevant.

Transforming solely to obtain p > 0.05 in Shapiro–Wilk

Transformation should have statistical and interpretive justification.

Reporting only p-values

Report effect sizes and uncertainty.

Treating correlation as causation

A relationship does not establish mechanism.

Removing outliers automatically

Investigate them first.

Converting missing observations to zero

Missing and zero mean different things.

Treating statistical significance as ecological importance

Always ask how large the effect is.

## The scientific method

Scientific research is a careful and systematic investigation of the natural or social world. It can improve existing knowledge, test established ideas, identify patterns and relationships, or generate new knowledge. Scientific conclusions should be based on evidence collected and analysed using transparent and reproducible methods.

A scientific investigation generally involves:

- Observing a pattern or developing an idea;

- Expressing that idea as a research question or hypothesis;
  - a hypothesis is a testable explanation or prediction based on existing knowledge or observations;
  - where appropriate, state both:
    - the **null hypothesis:** no difference, effect or relationship;
    - the **alternative hypothesis:** a difference, effect or relationship exists;

- Deciding how the question or hypothesis can be tested through an appropriate study or experimental design;
  - define the sampling units, variables and measurements;
  - decide how observations will be replicated;
  - consider whether observations are independent;
  - use the resulting evidence to either:
    - **reject the null hypothesis**, or
    - **fail to reject the null hypothesis**;
  - scientific evidence can support a hypothesis, but it does not usually prove it with absolute certainty;

- Collecting a suitable and representative sample;
  - **population**: the larger group or process about which we want to make an inference  
  - **sample**: the subset of observations actually collected from that population or process;
  - the aim is for the sample to represent the population sufficiently well for the intended inference;

::: {.callout-important}
## Potential problems
  >
  > - even a randomly selected sample may, by chance, represent the population poorly;
  > - natural ecological variation may obscure an underlying effect or pattern;
  > - inadequate sample size, biased sampling or non-independent observations can weaken inference;
  
:::

- Analysing the data using appropriate statistical methods;

- Evaluating how strongly the evidence supports the original hypothesis or explanation;
  - Consider estimated effect: how big the pattern, difference, or relationship is.
  - Consider uncertainty: how precise that estimate is. This is often expressed using a standard error or confidence interval.
  - Consider statistical evidence: how compatible the data are with a null hypothesis or statistical model. This can include a p-value, likelihood ratio test, AIC comparison, or other model-based evidence.
  - Consider alternative explanations: other processes that could produce the same observed pattern.

- Refining the explanation and generating new questions where necessary.

Science is therefore **iterative**. A statistical result is not the end of the process. It contributes evidence, changes what we know, and often generates new questions.

# Descriptive vs Inferential Statistics

**Descriptive statistics** are used to summarise and describe the data that were actually collected. They help us understand the main features of a sample before attempting to draw wider conclusions. Examples include the number or percentage of observations in different categories, the mean or median, measures of variation such as the standard deviation or range, and graphical summaries such as histograms or boxplots.

**Inferential statistics** are used to move beyond the observed sample and make inferences about a larger population or ecological process. They help us assess whether an observed difference, relationship or pattern is likely to reflect something more general rather than variation specific to the sample that happened to be collected.

Descriptive and inferential statistics therefore serve different but complementary purposes. Descriptive statistics tell us what the sample looks like; inferential statistics help us evaluate what the sample may tell us about the wider population or process.

Descriptive analysis usually comes first. After data have been collected, they should be inspected, summarised and visualised before formal statistical models or hypothesis tests are applied. This helps reveal patterns, variability, unusual observations and potential problems in the dataset.

# Hypotheses and null hypotheses

A useful hypothesis should be specific enough to test.

Weak:

> Fish abundance differs through time.

More useful:

> Fish abundance has declined through time.

The null hypothesis generally represents **no pattern or no difference**.

For example:

$$
H_0:
\text{there is no temporal trend in fish abundance}
$$

The alternative might be:

$$
H_1:
\text{fish abundance changes through time}
$$

or, if there is strong prior justification:

$$
H_1:
\text{fish abundance declines through time}
$$

::: {.callout-note}
## Directional prediction versus statistical test

A biologically directional prediction does not mean you should automatically use a one-sided test.

The scientific prediction and the formal test need to be considered separately.
:::

# Sampling effort and representativeness

A sample is intended to tell us something about a larger population or process.

The key question is:

> Is the sample representative enough for the inference I want to make?

## Replication

Replication allows variation to be estimated.

One observation may describe one event, but it gives almost no information about natural variability.

## Sample size

Sample size is the number of independent observational units contributing information to an analysis.

For example:

```text
20 independent surveys
→ n = 20
```

Sample size matters because it affects how precisely we can estimate ecological patterns.

Small sample:

- estimates are more variable;
- confidence intervals are wider;
- unusual observations have more influence;
- statistical tests have less power to detect real effects.

Larger sample:

- estimates are usually more stable;
- confidence intervals become narrower;
- patterns are easier to distinguish from random variation;
- the analysis has greater statistical power.

### Standard error

As sample size *n* increases, the standard error generally becomes smaller. This means the estimated mean becomes more precise.

### Statistical power

Statistical power is the probability of detecting a real effect when that effect actually exists.

Power generally increases when:

- sample size increases;
- the true effect is larger;
- variability is lower;
- the design is more efficient.

A study with very small sample size may fail to detect an ecologically meaningful effect simply because there is too much uncertainty.

### Ecological independence

In ecological datasets, the most important question is:

> How many independent sampling units do I have?


::: {.callout-important}
## Main learning goal

Very large sample sizes can make very small effects statistically significant.

With large *n*, always examine:

- effect size;
- confidence interval;
- ecological meaning.

:::


## Sampling effort

More effort generally gives a better picture of the system, but effort must also be comparable.

Examples:

- number of seine hauls;
- duration of kick sampling;
- quadrat area;
- number of visits.

### Random sampling

Sampling locations are selected using a random process.

Goal:

> reduce systematic selection bias.

### Systematic sampling

Samples are collected according to a structured design.

This is especially useful along environmental gradients.

Example:

> sampling from upper to lower estuary.

### Mixed sampling

Random and systematic elements are combined.

### Haphazard sampling

Sampling is not formally random or systematic.

This may sometimes be unavoidable, but limitations should be recognised.

# Independence and pseudoreplication

A major modern extension to basic test selection is the need to ask whether observations are truly independent.

Suppose you have:

```text
10 surveys at Richmond
10 surveys at Kew
10 surveys at Greenwich
```

You do **not** necessarily have 30 fully independent observations.

Surveys from the same site may resemble one another because they share:

- habitat;
- geography;
- salinity regime;
- local environmental conditions.

Repeated surveys within the same year may also share conditions.

This is hierarchical data - and this connects to the use of mixed models.

These are often confused.

## Difference 

### Two-group differences: t-test

The independent-samples **t-test** compares means between two groups with approximately Gaussian distribution, also called a normal distribution or bell curve.

Example dataset:

```{r}
two_sites <- survey_abundance %>%
  filter(
    SITE %in% c(
      "Richmond",
      "Kew"))
```

Plot first:

```{r}
ggplot(
  two_sites,
  aes(x = SITE, y = Log_Abundance)) +
  geom_boxplot() +
  geom_jitter(
    width = 0.1,
    alpha = 0.5) +
  theme_classic()
```

Fit:

```{r}
t.test(
  Log_Abundance ~ SITE,
  data = two_sites)
```

R uses Welch's t-test by default, which does not assume equal variances.

#### What the t-test asks

$$
H_0:
\mu_1 = \mu_2
$$

The test asks whether the observed difference in sample means is larger than would be expected from sampling variability under the null hypothesis.

#### What the p-value does not tell you

A small p-value does **not** tell you:

- the ecological importance of the difference;
- the probability that the null hypothesis is true;
- the magnitude of the effect.

Always inspect effect size and uncertainty.

### Two-group differences: Mann–Whitney U-test

For two non-parametric, independent groups where a rank-based comparison is more appropriate:

```{r}
wilcox.test(
  Abundance ~ SITE,
  data = two_sites)
```

In R this is implemented using `wilcox.test()`.

The test is based on ranks rather than raw values.

It is not simply a "test of medians" in every situation; its interpretation depends on distributional assumptions.

#### Paired tests

Paired data arise when observations are naturally matched.

Examples:

- before versus after at the same site;
- upstream versus downstream paired locations;
- same experimental unit under two conditions.

The pairing must be preserved.

Paired t-test:

```{r}
# t.test(
#   before,
#   after,
#   paired = TRUE)
```

Wilcoxon signed-rank test:

```{r}
# wilcox.test(
#   before,
#   after,
#   paired = TRUE)
```

Pairing is a design feature, not something to decide after seeing the data.


### More than two-group differences: ANOVA

ANOVA extends the logic of the t-test to more than two groups with Gaussian distribution.

For demonstration:

```{r}
m_site_aov <- aov(
  Log_Abundance ~ SITE,
  data = survey_abundance)

summary( m_site_aov)
```

The null hypothesis is:

$$
H_0:
\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k
$$

A significant ANOVA tells that at least one mean differs.

It does not identify which pair differs.

#### Post-hoc comparison

```{r}
TukeyHSD(m_site_aov)
```

Tukey's procedure performs pairwise comparisons while controlling family-wise error.

#### ANOVA assumptions

Classical ANOVA assumes:

- independent observations;
- approximately normal residuals;
- broadly homogeneous residual variance.

Check residuals:

```{r}
plot(
  fitted(m_site_aov),
  residuals(m_site_aov),
  xlab = "Fitted values",
  ylab = "Residuals")

abline(h = 0, lty = 2)

qqnorm(residuals(m_site_aov))

qqline(residuals(m_site_aov))
```

### More than two-group differences: Kruskal–Wallis test

A rank-based alternative for comparing more than two groups with non-parametric distribution:

```{r}
kruskal.test(
  Abundance ~ SITE,
  data = survey_abundance)
```

A significant result indicates that distributions differ among groups.

Follow-up comparisons require an appropriate multiple-comparison procedure.


## Correlation

Correlation asks:

> Do two variables change together?

Correlation does not establish causation.

#### Pearson correlation

Pearson correlation measures the strength of a **linear** association.

First create annual summary values for demonstration:

```{r}
annual_abundance <- survey_abundance %>%
  group_by(YEAR) %>%
  summarise(
    Mean_Log_Abundance = mean(Log_Abundance),
    .groups = "drop")
```

Plot:

```{r}
ggplot(
  annual_abundance,
  aes(
    x = YEAR,
    y = Mean_Log_Abundance)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(
    method = "lm",
    se = TRUE) +
  theme_classic()
```

Test:

```{r}
cor.test(
  annual_abundance$YEAR,
  annual_abundance$Mean_Log_Abundance,
  method = "pearson")
```

Pearson's \(r\) ranges from:

```text
-1  perfect negative linear association
 0  no linear association
+1  perfect positive linear association
```

#### Spearman rank correlation

Spearman correlation is based on ranks.

```{r}
cor.test(
  annual_abundance$YEAR,
  annual_abundance$Mean_Log_Abundance,
  method = "spearman",
  exact = FALSE)
```

Spearman is useful for monotonic relationships that are not necessarily straight lines.

#### Correlation versus regression

Correlation treats the two variables symmetrically.

Regression distinguishes:

- response;
- predictor.

For example:

```{r}
m_year <- lm(Mean_Log_Abundance ~ YEAR,
             data = annual_abundance)

summary(m_year)
```

Regression gives a slope:

> expected change in the response per unit change in the predictor.

### Nonlinear relationships

A low Pearson correlation does not mean there is no relationship.

A strong U-shaped relationship can have correlation near zero.

Always plot the data.

A quadratic example:

```{r}
m_quadratic <- lm(
  Mean_Log_Abundance ~
    YEAR +
    I(YEAR^2),
  data = annual_abundance)

summary(m_quadratic)
```

Only use nonlinear terms where they are scientifically defensible.


Inspect:

#### Goodness-of-fit test

A goodness-of-fit test compares observed categorical counts with an expected distribution.

Example:

```{r}
observed <- c(
  30,
  45,
  25)

expected_probs <- c(
  1/3,
  1/3,
  1/3)

chisq.test(
  observed,
  p = expected_probs)
```

Question:

> Do observed frequencies differ from the specified expectation?

## Prediction / Modelling

### Multiple regression

Multiple regression relates one continuous response to several predictors.

Prepare variables:

```{r}
survey_abundance <- survey_abundance %>%
  mutate(YEAR_CENTERED = YEAR - mean(YEAR),
    MONTH_FACTOR = factor(MONTH))
```

Fit:

```{r}
m_multiple <- lm(
  Log_Abundance ~ YEAR_CENTERED +
    MONTH_FACTOR,
  data = survey_abundance)

summary(m_multiple)
```

Interpretation:

- `YEAR_CENTERED` estimates temporal change while holding month constant;
- month coefficients compare months with the reference month.

This is the basis of the abundance model used later in the mixed-model workflow.

#### Multiple regression and confounding

Suppose sampling month changed systematically through time.

A raw relationship between abundance and year could partly reflect month.

Including both predictors asks:

> What is the year association after accounting for month?

This is one reason multivariable models are powerful.

But adding variables indiscriminately is not good statistical practice.

Predictors should be selected using biological reasoning and study design.

Create a demonstration occurrence variable:

# Reporting statistical results

Statistical reporting should answer four questions:

1. What was analysed?
2. What was the estimated pattern or difference?
3. How uncertain was the estimate?
4. What statistical evidence supports it?

Weak:

> Abundance significantly declined.

Better:

> Model-adjusted fish abundance declined through time, with an estimated annual change of X% (95% CI: X to X%; p < 0.001).

## Report effect sizes, not only p-values

A *p*-value answers a limited question about compatibility with a null model.

It does not tell you whether an effect is biologically important.

Whenever possible report:

- coefficient;
- difference;
- correlation;
- odds ratio;
- percentage change;
- confidence interval;
- relevant test statistic;
- p-value.

## Statistical significance versus biological importance

Consider:

```text
Effect A:
change = 0.1%
p < 0.001

Effect B:
change = 15%
p = 0.08
```

Effect A is statistically precise but may be ecologically trivial.

Effect B may be biologically important but uncertain.

Scientific interpretation requires both magnitude and uncertainty.

## Reporting graphs

A good figure should:

- answer a clear question;
- have labelled axes;
- show units;
- display uncertainty where relevant;
- avoid unnecessary decoration;
- be readable without excessive explanation.

Do not use a graph merely because software can produce it.

## Writing the Results section

Results should primarily describe evidence.

A useful paragraph structure is:

1. state the main pattern;
2. give the effect size;
3. give uncertainty;
4. give the test/model statistic;
5. refer to the figure or table.

Avoid explaining ecological mechanisms in detail here. That belongs mainly in the Discussion.

## Writing the Discussion

A strong Discussion separates:

```text
Result
↓
Biological interpretation
↓
Comparison with literature
↓
Alternative explanations
↓
Limitations
↓
Implications
```

For every interpretation ask:

> Did my analysis actually test this?

# Critical thinking

## Observation versus interpretation

Example:

**Observation**

> Fish abundance declined through time.

**Interpretation**

> Environmental conditions may have become less favourable.

**Causal claim**

> Declining oxygen caused the abundance decline.

The first may be directly supported.

The second may be plausible.

The third requires direct evidence.

Do not collapse these three levels.

## Alternative explanations

Whenever you favour an explanation, ask:

> What else could produce the same pattern?

For long-term fish monitoring:

- sampling month;
- gear differences;
- sampling effort;
- taxonomic changes;
- extreme recruitment years;
- site composition;
- habitat alteration;
- salinity change;
- temperature;
- water quality;
- stochastic variability.

Ask whether competing explanations have been eliminated.

## What would change my mind?

This is a powerful scientific habit.

Ask:

> What observation would weaken my preferred explanation?

If no possible evidence could change your conclusion, the conclusion is not being treated scientifically.

# Modern extensions

Modern ecological datasets are often:

- unbalanced;
- hierarchical;
- repeated;
- non-Gaussian.

Therefore the same concepts naturally extend to statistical models.

| Classical idea | Modern model framework |
|---|---|
| Compare means | LM / LMM |
| Count response | Poisson / negative-binomial GLM or GLMM |
| Binary response | Binomial GLM / GLMM |
| Repeated sites | Random effects |
| Multiple predictors | Multivariable model |
| Adjustment for month | Fixed covariate/factor |
| Site clustering | Random intercept |

# Modelling

## Linear model

```{r}
m_abundance_lm <- lm(
  Log_Abundance ~
    YEAR_CENTERED +
    MONTH_FACTOR,
  data = survey_abundance)

summary(m_abundance_lm)
```

This asks:

> Can variation in log abundance be explained by year and month if observations are treated as independent?

## Why a mixed model may be needed

Repeated observations occur within sites. Therefore surveys from the same site may be correlated.

A mixed model can represent this structure.

```{r}
# library(lme4)
#
# m_abundance_lmm <- lmer(
#   Log_Abundance ~
#     YEAR_CENTERED +
#     MONTH_FACTOR +
#     (1 | SITE),
#   data = survey_abundance,
#   REML = TRUE)
```

This is conceptually the same statistical thinking as earlier chapters, but with the sampling hierarchy represented explicitly.

## GLMs for non-Gaussian responses

A GLM extends linear modelling to responses such as:

- binary data;
- counts;
- positive skewed responses.

Examples:

```text
Binomial → occurrence
Poisson → counts
Gamma → positive continuous skewed data
```

The family and link function become part of the model.

## Model diagnostics replace simplistic test selection

Modern analysis should not be reduced to:

```text
normal → parametric
not normal → non-parametric
```

Instead ask:

- Is the response distribution appropriate?
- Are residuals behaving reasonably?
- Is variance changing?
- Is there overdispersion or underdispersion?
- Are there excess zeros?
- Is the random-effects structure supported?
- Does the model represent the design?


# A modern ecological analysis workflow

1. Define the biological question.
2. Define the sampling unit.
3. Identify response type.
4. Identify predictors.
5. Identify hierarchy/repeated sampling.
6. Explore the raw data.
7. Plot the response.
8. Decide whether transformation or a non-Gaussian family is appropriate.
9. Fit a biologically plausible model.
10. Diagnose assumptions.
11. Compare appropriate candidate models if needed.
12. Estimate effects and uncertainty.
13. Back-transform where required.
14. Visualise adjusted effects.
15. Interpret biological meaning.
16. Consider alternative explanations.
17. Report effect size, CI and statistical evidence.

## Classical tests and their model equivalents

Many familiar tests are special cases of regression models.

| Classical test | Equivalent model idea |
|---|---|
| One-sample t-test | Intercept-only LM |
| Two-sample t-test | LM with two-level factor |
| Paired t-test | Model differences / account for pairing |
| ANOVA | LM with categorical predictor |
| Pearson correlation | Closely related to simple linear regression |
| Logistic regression | Binomial GLM |

Understanding models therefore unifies many apparently separate statistical tests.

# Common mistakes

## Choosing the test before defining the question

Start with biology.

## Ignoring sampling design

Repeated observations are not automatically independent.

## Testing raw-response normality mechanically

For models, residual behaviour is usually more relevant.

## Transforming solely to obtain *p > 0.05* in Shapiro–Wilk

Transformation should have statistical and interpretive justification.

## Reporting only *p-values*

Report effect sizes and uncertainty.

## Treating correlation as causation

A relationship does not establish mechanism.

## Removing outliers automatically

Investigate them first.

## Converting missing observations to zero

Missing and zero mean different things.

## Treating statistical significance as ecological importance

Always ask how large the effect is.
